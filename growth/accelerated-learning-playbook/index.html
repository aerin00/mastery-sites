Start here

Optimize for what you can still do later, on a task you have never seen, with AI switched off. Use AI to create practice, diagnose errors, and give hints. Score your first independent attempt before asking for help. Then redo the skill on a fresh task after a delay.

This playbook assumes one primary skill, 12 weeks, and about 4 hours a week. The goal is a demonstrable improvement on a bounded task. It can also run at 2 or 6 hours a week. Pick one track for the first cycle. The evidence review is a targeted synthesis of primary studies and selected reviews through November 2026. The combined 12-week protocol, score thresholds, and review intervals are operating defaults designed for this guide. Their exact settings have no independent validation as a package.

Today, spend 45 minutes on three things. Write a goal with a visible output, reserve an independent assessment, and take the baseline in Diagnostics. After that, schedule your first four sessions.

What the evidence supports

Strong means converging evidence for the stated outcome. Conditional means the task, learner, or implementation materially changes the result. Mixed means findings diverge or a proposed general effect has failed to replicate. Weak means evidence is thin for the claim as commonly sold. Ratings apply to the specific outcome in each row.

Method Verdict What to do
Retrieval practice, recalling from memory Strong for delayed retention. Transfer is more conditional. In prose experiments, restudy won at 5 minutes while prior recall won after 2 days or a week [1]. Benefits also appeared on inference questions [2], and repeated retrieval helped retain foreign vocabulary [3]. Answer before opening notes. Use free recall and short answers. Also retrieve explanations, decisions and solution steps.
Distributed practice, revisiting across days Strong for retention. The useful gap varies with how long you want to remember and what you are learning [4]. Put reviews on different days. Keep the exact intervals adjustable.
A universal expanding review schedule Mixed. Expanding gaps helped on a 10-minute vocabulary test in one series of experiments, while equally spaced practice did better at two days. The timing of the first retrieval mattered [5]. Use a manageable schedule and watch delayed recall.
Worked examples and fading, gradually removing steps Strong for novice structured problem solving, conditional on prior knowledge. Algebra experiments found benefits from studying solved problems [6]. Apprentice studies found that extra guidance can lose its advantage as expertise rises [7]. Study one solved example, explain why each step works, fill in missing steps, then solve a fresh problem.
Interleaving, mixing related problem types Conditional, strong in some selection tasks. In a randomized study of 54 seventh-grade math classes, scores one month later averaged 61% with interleaving and 38% with blocked practice [8]. Painting-style experiments and a meta-analysis show why similarity and task type matter [9, 25]. Mix confusable methods once each is usable. Have the learner name the clue that determined the choice.
Corrective feedback Strong for correcting specific errors, conditional on design. Experiments found feedback improved retention and reduced uptake of wrong multiple-choice options. It also helped preserve correct answers given with low confidence [10, 11]. Mark the first error, explain the rule, and require a fresh attempt. Treat vague praise as encouragement.
Self-explanation, including the “Feynman technique” Conditional. Prompted self-explanation improved understanding in primary experiments. The branded routine as a whole has much thinner evidence [12]. Explain why a step works and when it would fail, then verify your explanation against a source.
Meaningful words plus diagrams Conditional. Controlled multimedia experiments found better problem solving with coordinated narration and animation in the task tested [13]. Use a diagram when it shows a mechanism or relationship. Reconstruct it from memory and explain the arrows.
Keyword mnemonics and memory aids Conditional and narrow. An early keyword experiment showed benefits for learning vocabulary [14]. Remembering a translation and using a word fluently are different outcomes. Use memory aids for arbitrary associations. Add sentence production, listening and conversation.
Highlighting, rereading, passive videos and polished AI summaries Weak as the main strategy for durable learning. Restudy can improve immediate recall and confidence [1]. Reviews rate rereading and highlighting as low-utility defaults [24]. Use them to orient yourself or repair a gap. Close the source and produce an answer or solve a problem.
Cramming and massed drills Conditional. Closely grouped practice can help immediate performance. Distributed sessions support longer retention [1, 4]. Batch a few first attempts when a procedure is new. Revisit on later days.
Matching teaching to a “visual” or “auditory” learner Weak. A study that tested the matching prediction found no matching benefit for comprehension [15]. Pick the format that fits the content and the learner’s accessibility needs.
Handwriting always beats typing Mixed. Original experiments found a conceptual advantage for longhand. A direct replication did not reproduce that advantage [16, 17]. Use a medium you can work in easily. Convert the notes into questions and answer them from memory.
Brain-training games for general intelligence Weak for broad transfer. An action-game experiment found improvements on specific visual-attention measures. Reviews find stronger evidence for trained or similar tasks than for broad everyday gains [18, 26]. Train the actual target skill. Assess any hoped-for transfer directly.
Speed-reading with full comprehension Weak for large speed gains at fixed comprehension. The review literature describes a trade-off when reading much faster [27]. Skim to locate information, then slow down for arguments, derivations and technical details. Check comprehension afterward.
The 10,000-hour rule Weak as a universal threshold. Mixed on how much accumulated practice explains expertise. The original violin study relied in part on retrospective estimates. A preregistered replication did not find more accumulated practice among the best group than the next group [19, 20]. Design practice around specific bottlenecks. Track demonstrated performance and seek expert feedback when possible.

What memory and expertise research add

  • Encoding, retention and retrieval are different problems. Encoding is forming an initial representation. Retention is keeping what you learned across a delay. Retrieval is accessing it when needed. If a learner cannot explain an example immediately, improve instruction. If they can explain today and fail next week, add spaced retrieval. If they remember a rule and choose the wrong rule in a new task, add contrasting cases. Transfer means applying what you know to a different task or setting. In four experiments, repeated testing improved some transfer outcomes over restudy. A meta-analysis found transfer varied by what was practiced and how it was tested [28, 29].
  • Sleep research supports consolidation, the stabilization of new memories. In a controlled experiment, reintroducing a previously paired odour during slow-wave sleep improved later recall of a spatial declarative-memory task. The same benefit did not extend to the procedural task studied [21]. Juggling training produced changes detectable by brain imaging [22]. These are specific findings about memory and experience-dependent brain change. They provide no study-derived dosage for this plan. Put practice across normal waking days and preserve sleep time.
  • Expertise is often specific to meaningful patterns. A small classic chess experiment found a larger recall advantage for skilled players on meaningful positions than on random ones [23]. Learn the patterns that change a decision in your field. Ask what signals a technique, what would fool you and what happens when a constraint changes.
  • Deliberate practice targets a weakness with repeat attempts and feedback. For a broad self-study version, isolate one component, choose a task just beyond current ability, get specific feedback, retry and increase complexity as performance improves. The narrower expertise literature emphasizes training designed with skilled guidance. A meta-analysis found practice explained different shares of performance differences across domains. The studies largely measure associations, and the definition of practice is contested [19, 20, 30, 31]. Coaching access, prior preparation, opportunity and measurement can all matter.
  • Fluency feels like learning. In a randomized college physics classroom experiment, active learners learned more while reporting that they felt they had learned less [32]. A review of learning versus performance documents similar dissociations [33]. Hard practice can also be unproductive when prerequisites are missing. Let later independent performance guide difficulty.

How to read the evidence

Primary experiments support causal claims within their tested materials, populations, comparisons and outcome windows. A lab task with word pairs offers a clean memory test. A classroom trial offers stronger evidence that an approach can work amid ordinary instruction. Neither alone establishes how a self-directed adult will perform on a novel workplace task months later. This is a targeted review, so it cannot estimate an overall effect size or rule out publication bias. Where possible, inspect the comparison group, time on task, scoring conditions, attrition and delayed or transfer outcomes before copying an intervention. A null result can reflect an imprecise estimate or low uptake. Results from different controls and tests cannot be ranked by their headline effect sizes.

What AI tutoring adds

Primary study What was measured What it supports
Bastani et al., 2025 [34], field experiment with nearly 1,000 high-school math students Compared with no AI, practice grades rose 48% for an unrestricted GPT-4 interface and 127% for a safeguarded tutor. On a subsequent unaided test, the unrestricted group scored 17% lower than control. The safeguarded design largely mitigated the penalty. These figures are relative changes. Track assisted practice and independent learning separately. Use verified problem solutions and hint-based tutoring.
Kestin et al., 2025 [35], randomized crossover with 194 enrolled undergraduate physics students A purpose-built AI tutor yielded larger short-term test gains in less time than the classroom active-learning condition on the lessons studied. A carefully designed tutor can help with bounded lessons. Long-term retention and broader transfer remain open here.
Wang et al., 2025 [36], working paper, randomized human tutoring study Giving human tutors access to AI guidance raised the probability of session-level math-topic mastery by 4 percentage points. Human-led AI support can improve this immediate instructional outcome.
Oreopoulos & Low, 2026 [37], working paper, two-year cluster trial across 18 middle schools Khan Academy with Khanmigo improved math achievement under the study comparison. Gains resembled estimates for Khan Academy practice without AI, and substantive chat use was infrequent. The study offers limited grounds for attributing gains specifically to the chatbot. Measure how the learner actually uses it.
Oreopoulos et al., 2026 [38], working paper, randomized middle-school math study with over 6,000 students AI raised in-session answer accuracy, with fewer questions attempted. The clearest delayed-test signal was marginal and concentrated on practiced material when AI was embedded in a mastery workflow. Better next attempts and throughput are different outcomes. Examine delayed gains and task coverage.

Traditional intelligent tutoring systems also have evidence from controlled studies synthesized in meta-analysis [39]. Those systems typically follow specific domains and feedback rules. Language models offer flexible dialogue and exercise generation. Their outputs still require checking. Adaptive review can estimate forgetting from past responses. A Duolingo study reported better recall prediction and more engagement for its model [40]. Those measures alone cannot establish broader language proficiency.

Set up four components

  1. Source of truth. Pick one main textbook, course, official reference, or verified expert rubric. Keep its relevant chapters, examples or rules together.
  2. Tutor chat. Paste the tutor contract, your goal, the allowed sources and the current skill. Use the tutor for explanations, hints, drills and feedback.
  3. Review queue. Use a flashcard app or a simple list with item, answer/source, last score and next due date. Keep factual cards separate from longer problem prompts.
  4. Assessment bank and log. Keep held-out tasks away from your tutor and practice materials. Use official questions, tests prepared by another person, or a separate AI author with independently verified answers. Keep scores in one log and store the evidence behind them.

Verify with the right tool. Run code against tests. Check mathematics against a worked solution, substitution, a counterexample, or a qualified reviewer when a proof is in dispute. Use a fluent speaker or trusted audio for language nuance. Check a business claim against the supplied data. For technical procedures, use approved references and a sandbox. If the model and your reference disagree, mark the item unresolved until checked.

Diagnostics and measurement

Establish a baseline

  1. Write an outcome in observable form. “By week 12, I can write and test a Python CSV summarizer in 45 minutes, using the standard-library docs and no AI, including malformed input and an unfamiliar schema.”
  2. Map four to six subskills and their prerequisites. Mark each as knowledge, procedure or judgment. Choose one central task and one changed-context task.
  3. Reserve comparable forms A (baseline), B (week 4), C (week 8), D (week 12) and E (follow-up). Keep the blueprint, scoring and time limit stable. Change values, scenarios and surface wording. Check that forms have comparable difficulty. Record any differences. Prepare new independent checks during the cycle without reusing reserved items.
  4. Run a 45-minute assessment. Spend 8 minutes on six short closed-book retrieval items, 12 minutes on two short applications, 20 minutes on one task in a different context, and 5 minutes recording confidence and scoring notes. Where the skill requires tools, predeclare the permitted reference or software and use the same rules across forms. Capture the first answer before looking at feedback.
  5. Route the result. Cannot explain or start: repair prerequisites using a worked example. Can start, fails steps: drill the earliest faulty step. Can execute when cued, chooses wrong method when mixed: interleave similar tasks. Can solve familiar tasks, fails the changed case: practice variants and explain what changed. Can solve the changed case: raise one constraint or shorten time after accuracy stabilizes.

Rubric

Score each application or transfer task on correctness, method selection, reasoning/verification, and completion under the stated constraints. Each criterion is 0 (missing or wrong), 1 (partly correct or incomplete) or 2 (correct and independently demonstrated), for a total of 8. Add one field for a critical error that must be absent, such as silently corrupting data, relying on a false premise, or ignoring a safety constraint. For a short factual recall item, use 1 for correct and 0 otherwise. Score confidence before feedback as 0–100%.

Indicator How to measure Suggested decision
7-day retrieval Correct on first attempt ÷ items assessed about seven days after last exposure. Record the actual gap and item count. Aim for at least 80% across two checks. If lower, shorten gaps or relearn weak items.
Independent application Points earned ÷ points possible on two new applications, with allowed tools only. Aim for 13/16 or higher before adding complexity.
Transfer Fresh scenario on the /8 rubric, without topic labels or hints. Aim for 6/8 or higher on two distinct variants, including the week-12 or follow-up task, with no critical errors.
Hint dependence Attempts requiring a hint or solution ÷ eligible first attempts of comparable difficulty. If flat or rising for two weeks, reduce task complexity and fade help in smaller steps.
Calibration Mean confidence minus actual accuracy on binary-scored items. Also count wrong answers rated at least 80% confident. Inspect high-confidence mistakes first. A gap of 15 percentage points across repeated checks is a review trigger.
Speed at quality Median completion time across correct comparable tasks. Track only after correctness is stable. Keep time spent and completed sessions as context.

These targets are defaults. Set them before starting and adjust for a real standard when one exists. Report raw counts and dates. Two tests with very few items are a weak basis for inferring stable skill. A 7-day test should use a small set held aside from intervening review, or report the gap since last review honestly. A 28-day follow-up measures persistence after normal use unless you also hold that subset out for 28 days.

Example. A learner moves from 7/16 to 13/16 on application and from 2/8 to 5/8 on transfer. The week-12 transfer hurdle is still open. If 8/10 recall items are correct at seven days and average confidence is 90%, the calibration gap is +10 percentage points. The next intervention is a pair of contrasting transfer cases and a fresh transfer test a week later.

Sample diagnostic with a scoring key

Use this probability assessment to rehearse the process. Reserve separate items for your actual baseline.

  • Recall, six points. Define an event, a joint event, a conditional probability, a complement, independence and a base rate. Award one point per valid definition.
  • Application 1, eight points. Of 40 students, 18 study French and six study both French and Spanish. A randomly chosen student studies French. What is the chance they study Spanish?
  • Application 2, eight points. Two independent alerts each have a 10% daily probability of firing. What is the probability at least one fires? Explain how independence enters.
  • Transfer, eight points. Of 1,000 transactions, 20 are duplicates. A rule flags 15 of those and 49 other transactions. Compare the fraction of flags that are duplicates with the fraction of duplicates detected. Propose one check before changing the rule.
Sample diagnostic key

Recall: an event is a set of outcomes. A joint event requires both events. A conditional probability restricts the reference group to the conditioning event. A complement contains outcomes outside the event. Independence means the joint probability factors into the product of the individual probabilities. A base rate is the overall frequency or probability in the relevant population.

Application 1: 6/18 = 1/3. Application 2: 1 − 0.9 × 0.9 = 19%. Transfer: 15/64 ≈ 23.4% of flags are duplicates, while 15/20 = 75% of duplicates are detected. One next check is to inspect the five missed duplicates and test a revised rule on fresh data. Confusing the two denominators is a critical error in this case.

The 12-week framework

Default rhythm, four hours. Monday 50 minutes for a new skill, Wednesday 50 for repair or variation, Friday 50 for mixed practice, Saturday 70 for an independent assessment and project, and 20 minutes of short review on other days. In a two-hour week, use two 35-minute sessions, one 35-minute check/project block and 15 minutes of review. At six hours, add a 60-minute case and 60 minutes of feedback or live practice. Cut the number of target subskills when time shrinks.

50-minute practice session. 8 minutes of due retrieval, 10 minutes for a verified example or a prior error, 22 minutes of practice with an independent first attempt, 7 minutes for correction and a retry, and 3 minutes to log and schedule. For a 35-minute session, use 5 / 7 / 16 / 5 / 2 minutes. On assessment days, freeze the independent result before opening the tutor.

Week Aim and work Proof of progress
1 Define one outcome, map prerequisites, prepare forms, take baseline A. Learn the first weak prerequisite from a verified example. Baseline scores, first error log and review queue.
2 Build the first core procedure. Study a worked example, complete a partial example, then solve a similar problem independently. Two fresh first attempts plus a seven-day check on week 1 material.
3 Add a second procedure. Compare one pair that looks similar but requires different methods. Written selection rule and one new problem per method.
4 Take form B under baseline conditions. Repair the most frequent or highest-impact error. Scores compared with A and a fresh retry scheduled 1–3 days later.
5 Interleave three problem types in an unlabeled set. Explain the reason for each choice. Count of correct method selections, including cases with plausible distractors.
6 Change one surface feature or constraint. Try the problem first, then request the smallest hint if needed. One near-transfer score and a record of which changes caused mistakes.
7 Start a small authentic project. Isolate its weakest component for repeated drills and obtain external or objective feedback. First project draft, verified test results or reviewer comments.
8 Take form C. Reallocate practice based on the diagnostic pattern. Midpoint-to-late comparison and one concrete change to the practice plan.
9 Add ambiguity or missing information. State assumptions, test them, and update the answer. A scored unfamiliar case and a short account of what changed your choice.
10 Rehearse under target conditions, including tools and time. Build a fresh mini-project from a blank start. Comparable time at stable accuracy, plus one independent review.
11 Fix the last persistent bottleneck. Run a parallel rehearsal and keep form D held out. One delayed independent retry and a final capstone checklist.
12 Take form D and complete or defend the capstone. Compare with A, B and C. Schedule follow-up form E for four weeks later. Final scores, remaining weakness, and a decision to advance or repeat one component.

If you miss a week, run a short retrieval and application check before resuming. Move missed reviews into the next available sessions. When time is limited, prioritize due items that support the main goal and preserve the independent checkpoint.

For a missed review, relearn the smallest failing piece, retry after another item, then schedule it for the next day and three days later if the next-day attempt succeeds. Resume longer intervals after success. For partial recall, review in 1–3 days. For clear independent success, move to the next longer planned gap. Cards that earn their place. One cue should have one scorable response. Include the source and a cue that forces production. Good prompts ask when a rule applies, distinguish two similar ideas, or predict an outcome. For complex work, use cards for building blocks and reserve complete exercises for integration.

Practice packs for five subjects

Try the prompts before opening the answer keys. These are training examples. Use independently verified parallel tasks for formal checkpoints.

Programming · Python data processing

Target: Build a CSV summarizer that normalizes categories, validates values and reports skipped rows. Diagnostic: Predict what changes when a dictionary key appears twice, write a five-line aggregation loop, and handle an unseen malformed row without losing the valid rows.

Drill: For these rows, sum numeric amounts by lowercase, trimmed category and count invalid amounts. Treat blank amounts as invalid.

Travel,8
Food,oops
Travel,

Write expected results first. Implement and test empty input, an invalid row and multiple categories. Transfer: Extend the spec to allow quoted commas in category names and refunds. Use a fresh fixture. Decide which behaviours require a test before editing. Capstone: Produce a runnable command-line tool with a short README and verified tests. Use the standard-library documentation as the declared reference. Score independently by tests and a short explanation of failure handling.

Programming answer and scoring notes

Food totals 12.50, Travel totals 8.00 and there are two invalid amounts. In a Python dictionary literal with duplicate equal keys, the last value wins. Updating a running total explicitly accumulates both values. A CSV parser is needed for quoted fields. Include a test for a category such as “Travel, local”, and define how negative amounts affect totals. If exact money arithmetic is part of the spec, check the use of decimal values.

Mathematics · Choosing a probability model

Target: Identify and compute conditional probabilities from tables or counts and explain your denominator. Diagnostic: Distinguish “A given B” from “B given A,” compute a basic proportion and draw a small frequency table.

Drill: Among 100 people, 20 have a condition. Of those, 16 test positive. Eight of the 80 without the condition also test positive. Find the chance of testing positive given the condition, then the chance of having the condition given a positive result. Explain the two reference groups. Transfer: The test keeps the same sensitivity and false-positive rate, but prevalence falls to 5%. Use 200 people and recompute the second probability. Capstone: Interpret an unlabeled scenario, choose between conditional probability, joint probability and simple proportion, and defend the chosen denominator.

Mathematics answer and scoring notes

First result: 16/20 = 80%. Second: 16/(16 + 8) = 2/3. In the transfer scenario, 10 have the condition and 8 test positive. Of the other 190, 19 test positive. The resulting probability is 8/27, about 29.6%. Score the denominator and the interpretation separately from the arithmetic.

Business · Unit economics and decisions

Target: Write a decision memo that uses contribution margin, break-even volume and sensitivity to recommend a next action. Diagnostic: Distinguish fixed and per-unit costs, compute a margin and state one uncertainty that might change the decision.

Drill: A product sells for $50, costs $30 per unit to deliver and has $2,000 monthly fixed costs. Estimate break-even units and profit at 130 units. Transfer: A reseller keeps 20% of the selling price and variable delivery costs rise to $32. Recalculate and identify what you need to check before choosing the reseller. Capstone: Compare two routes to market using a fresh data set. Deliver a one-page recommendation with assumptions, sensitivity and a testable decision rule. Use a reviewer or independently checked spreadsheet for the arithmetic.

Business answer and scoring notes

Base contribution is $20 per unit. Break-even is 100 units and profit at 130 units is $600 under the stated assumptions. With the reseller, net revenue is $40 and contribution is $8 per unit. With fixed costs held constant, break-even is 250 units. A strong recommendation considers expected volume, how long the new terms hold, and whether other costs or sales channels change.

Languages · Handling a travel disruption in French

Target: Handle a three-minute exchange about a schedule change with understandable speech, one clarification and a correct summary of the plan. Diagnostic: Retrieve five relevant phrases, listen to a short verified recording twice, and respond aloud to an unexpected change. Record the response before seeking feedback.

Drill: You expected a 14:30 departure to Lyon. The speaker says, “Le train pour Lyon part à quinze heures vingt, voie quatre.” State the new time and platform, then ask if the original ticket is still valid. Have the AI or a partner vary times and platforms and use verified audio when assessing listening. Transfer: Your connection is threatened. Ask for an alternative and repeat back the new itinerary. Capstone: Complete a live role-play with an unfamiliar speaker or voice, record it, and score comprehension, intelligibility, successful clarification and task completion. Have a fluent speaker verify disputed phrasing when feasible.

French answer and scoring notes

The train leaves at 15:20 from platform 4. A valid response is “Le train part à quinze heures vingt, voie quatre. Est-ce que mon billet est toujours valable ?” Allow other comprehensible phrasings. Score whether the learner understands the change and asks the intended question. For actual listening assessment, use audio without showing the transcript.

Technical subjects · Network troubleshooting

Target: Diagnose an unfamiliar connectivity failure in a simulated environment and document the evidence and next test. Diagnostic: Explain what DNS does, name one symptom that distinguishes name resolution from routing and propose a reversible first check.

Drill: A known HTTPS endpoint fails by hostname on one workstation. A lookup for that name times out on the configured resolver. A request to the same endpoint succeeds when a verified IP is supplied while retaining the original hostname and TLS verification. What is your leading hypothesis and what two tests would you run? Transfer: DNS resolves correctly but the app returns a certificate-name mismatch. Change your hypothesis and propose a test that preserves certificate verification. Capstone: Resolve a simulated incident with at least one misleading symptom and submit a timeline, test outputs, root-cause confidence and rollback plan.

Technical answer and scoring notes

The original evidence points to the DNS resolution path. Compare another hostname on the same resolver and query the original name through an approved alternative resolver or a known-good workstation. A timeout alone leaves several possibilities, including resolver reachability or an upstream issue. In the changed case, inspect the certificate presented for the hostname and compare the configured name, server and proxy route with a known-good path. Preserve certificate verification.

Feedback and adaptation

After each task, run one correction loop. Freeze the original answer and confidence. Find the first consequential error. Classify it as missing knowledge, procedure, method selection, interpretation, execution or verification. Use a verified reference to resolve it. Ask for one cue and retry from a blank start on a parallel task. Schedule a delayed retry. When the same error returns, simplify the task until you can isolate the failing step.

Use a hint ladder. Level 0 is independent. Level 1 asks the learner to inspect a specific clue. Level 2 names the relevant principle or contrast. Level 3 provides a partial worked step. Level 4 shows a verified solution. Pause between levels for a learner attempt. Mark the maximum level used. Once a solution is shown, pick a fresh parallel item for independent scoring.

For subjective tasks, have a second scorer independently score occasional samples using the same rubric. Keep prompt versions, references and raw work when using AI to grade. Resolve material disagreements with a person or an objective check before treating them as a learning signal. If you want to see whether AI saves you time, alternate matched practice sets with and without AI. Keep practice time and difficulty comparable, then compare fresh delayed checks. A small personal trial can guide your workflow. Its results will be sensitive to task order and the items chosen.
