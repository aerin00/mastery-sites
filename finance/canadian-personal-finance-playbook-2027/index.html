2027 Personal Finance Playbook

Decide where your next dollar should go, choose accounts for your goals, and put the plan on a monthly schedule.

Start with your next dollar

  1. If cash flow is negative: cover housing, food, utilities, essential transport and coverage. Make required debt payments where possible. Address arrears and contact creditors early. Review benefits and income before committing to new contributions.
  2. If cash flow is positive but fragile: build a starter buffer based on a real shock, such as your largest likely deductible or one pay cycle of essential bills. Keep minimums current. Evaluate any employer match you can afford after checking vesting, fees and withdrawal restrictions.
  3. If expensive debt remains: direct extra payments to the highest effective rate after minimums, unless arrears or a near-term contractual deadline changes the order. Keep enough cash to avoid borrowing again at the first setback.
  4. If cash flow is stable: finish the emergency reserve and fund dated goals. Use FHSA for an eligible home goal, TFSA for flexibility or low current effective tax rates, and RRSP for retirement when the deduction or employer match makes it worthwhile. Test tax and benefit interactions before choosing.
  5. If the basics are funded: invest long-term money in a diversified portfolio you can hold through a downturn. Close insurance gaps and test how the plan behaves under job loss, a mortgage reset or poor early retirement returns.

These steps are a decision framework. A benefit deadline, a grant, or a particularly valuable match can move a goal up the list if the household can still pay its bills. [1] [2] [3]

Cash flow and emergency reserves

Use the last three months of transactions and a full year of irregular bills. Start with actual take-home pay and recurring benefits. For biweekly pay, multiply by 26 and divide by 12 to estimate the monthly average, then map real pay dates to bill dates. For irregular income, build the budget on a conservative low month and use stronger months to build reserves. Keep business taxes and any GST/HST collected in separate accounts from spending money. [1] [4]

Monthly worksheet What to enter
Reliable inflows Take-home pay, support and recurring benefits. Count uncertain income separately.
Essential outflows Housing, food, utilities, essential transport, dependent care, minimum debt payments and core insurance.
Predictable irregular bills Annual cost divided by 12 for items such as repairs, renewals and gifts. Set aside that amount each month.
Flexible outflows Discretionary spending, with a cap you can sustain.
Available for goals Inflows minus the three spending categories above. Give each dollar a job at the next payday.

FCAC suggests aiming for three to six months of regular expenses or income. An essential-expense target is a practical adaptation. Lean toward the higher end when one income carries the household, work is variable or benefits are limited. Stress-test a longer interruption where job replacement could take longer. Hold the first layer in accessible savings and consider cashable or staggered short guaranteed investment certificates (GICs) only for money you can wait to access. Check the institution, account category, ownership and redemption terms. CDIC covers eligible deposits, including principal and interest, up to $100,000 per insured category at each member institution. Provincial credit union schemes can differ. [2] [5]

Worked example. Take-home income of $4,200 minus $2,800 essentials, $300 irregular-bill funding and $500 flexible spending leaves $600 a month. Three and six months of essentials are $8,400 and $16,800. With $2,400 already saved and all $600 directed to the reserve, the targets take 10 and 24 months, respectively, ignoring interest. If the household has a $4,000 credit card balance at 20%, it can keep its starter buffer and decide how much of the $600 to divert to debt.

Payday checklist: ☐ Cover the next bills and minimums. ☐ Transfer tax and irregular-bill money. ☐ Fund the current priority automatically. ☐ Check upcoming account balances so timing does not trigger overdraft.

Debt and housing

Record each balance, annual rate, minimum, due date, collateral, renewal or promo expiry and prepayment penalty. Make minimums across debts and address arrears. With the avalanche method, direct extra money to the highest rate. With the snowball method, pay the smallest balance first if quick wins help you stick with the plan. For consolidation, compare total interest and fees over the same payoff period and check whether unsecured debt becomes secured against your home. If minimums are persistently unaffordable, contact creditors, compare reputable credit counselling, and consult a Licensed Insolvency Trustee about formal options such as a consumer proposal or bankruptcy. [3] [6] [7]

Worked example Calculation Decision
Two debts $1,000 extra against a 20% card cuts the next month’s interest by about $16.67. Against an 8% line of credit, about $6.67. Assumes monthly interest at annual rate ÷ 12 and otherwise equal timing. The card saves about $10 more in the first month.
Mortgage renewal $400,000 with 20 years remaining costs about $2,417 monthly at 4%, or $2,849 at 6%. Canadian nominal rates compounded semi-annually, monthly payments, principal and interest only. Test whether roughly $432 more per month fits. Ask about lender fees, amortization, prepayment rights and qualification.
Extra principal or savings A penalty-free prepayment at 4.5% on non-deductible debt can be compared with a taxable interest rate of 4.5% ÷ (1 − 30%) = 6.43% under a flat 30% marginal tax assumption. Preserve needed liquidity and confirm the mortgage terms. Investment returns carry risk and may vary.

For renewals, start comparing offers several months ahead. Confirm whether a switch qualifies for applicable straight-switch treatment with the receiving lender, and compare fees and contractual terms. For a purchase, budget closing costs, taxes, insurance, maintenance and, where applicable, condo or strata fees. Keep closing funds and the post-closing cash buffer separate. [8] [9]

Account and tax decisions

Choose the account for its tax treatment and access rules, then choose the holdings for the goal. A TFSA, RRSP or FHSA may hold cash or investments depending on the provider. An account label alone does not determine investment risk.

Account Contributions and room Withdrawals and best fit
TFSA · Tax-Free Savings Account No deduction. Unused room carries forward. Track deposits across providers using your records, since CRA figures can lag. Room depends on age and Canadian residence. Generally tax-free. Withdrawals return as room on January 1 of the next year. Use issuer-to-issuer transfers to move accounts. Flexible goals and retirement top-ups. Excess amounts generally attract 1% per month.
RRSP · Registered Retirement Savings Plan Deductible up to the personal limit when claimed. New room generally starts with the lesser of 18% of prior-year earned income and the annual ceiling, adjusted for pension items. Unused room can carry forward. Check the Notice of Assessment and previously reported undeducted contributions. Ordinary withdrawals are taxable and generally use up the room permanently. Withholding is a prepayment. Useful for an employer match or when the effective deduction rate exceeds the expected effective withdrawal rate.
FHSA · First Home Savings Account $8,000 initial room when the first account opens, $40,000 lifetime limit and up to $8,000 of unused participation room carried forward. Eligible cash contributions are generally deductible. Direct RRSP transfers use FHSA room and give no new deduction. Qualifying first-home withdrawals are tax-free with no repayment. Other withdrawals are generally taxable. An eligible direct transfer to your RRSP or RRIF generally uses no RRSP room.
Taxable account No registered contribution ceiling or deduction. Track adjusted cost base (ACB), the tax cost used to calculate a gain, in Canadian dollars across relevant holdings. Interest, dividends and realized gains have different tax treatment. Useful after suitable registered room is used or when registered access rules do not fit.

FHSA eligibility to open and to withdraw has distinct first-time-buyer tests. Check Canadian residency, age, prior homes and any spouse or partner history that applies. The FHSA deadline is based on the earliest of the 15th anniversary year, the year you turn 71, or the year after your first qualifying withdrawal. FHSA contributions for a deduction follow the calendar year. An RRSP deduction can generally include eligible contributions made in the first 60 days of the following year. [10] [11] [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] [17]

The Home Buyers’ Plan (HBP) permits up to $60,000 per eligible person from RRSPs. You can combine it with an FHSA withdrawal for the same home if you meet both sets of conditions. CRA currently shows a fifth-year repayment start for first withdrawals made in 2022–2028. A first withdrawal in 2027 therefore starts repayment in 2032. A $30,000 withdrawal initially implies $2,000 a year over 15 years, subject to CRA’s statement and any early repayment. A missed required amount is generally included in income. [18] [19]

Worked TFSA vs. RRSP example. Start with a $3,000 after-tax budget and a 40% effective deduction rate. A $5,000 RRSP contribution costs $3,000 after a $2,000 tax saving if the full deduction is usable and you can fund the contribution or arrange payroll relief. If both accounts grow 50%, the TFSA ends at $4,500. The RRSP reaches $7,500 before tax and leaves $5,625 at a 25% withdrawal rate, $4,500 at 40%, or $3,750 at 50%. Growth and rates are hypothetical. Compare full tax and benefit outcomes across brackets and years. [13] [15]

Worked FHSA example. An eligible person contributes $8,000 in 2027 and can deduct it at a constant 30% marginal rate, for about $2,400 in tax savings. If the account earns $240, a qualifying withdrawal provides $8,240 for the home. The $2,400 tax saving is separate and can be saved if cash flow allows. Check eligibility and leave time for paperwork and access to the funds. [14]

Worked taxable example. Suppose a resident earns $400 of fully taxable interest and realizes a $2,000 net capital gain after selling costs. Under a one-half inclusion assumption and a flat 30% marginal rate, estimated tax is $120 on interest plus $300 on the gain, or $420, before credits or benefit effects. Recheck the inclusion rate and the treatment of the transaction in the year of sale. For funds, track reinvested taxable distributions and return of capital in your ACB records. Check the superficial-loss rules before selling and repurchasing for a tax loss. [20] [21]

File annually and check the benefit effect of a deduction or taxable withdrawal. Use the relevant federal and provincial rules and your combined marginal rate, the tax on the next dollar, with any changes in income-tested benefits. Canada Child Benefit (CCB) uses adjusted family net income. CRA says the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit (CGEB) replaced the GST/HST credit in July 2026. Compare current and future rates with benefit estimates when deciding whether to claim or carry forward an eligible deduction. [22] [23] [24]

Choose priorities by household and goal

The amounts below describe hypothetical households. A gross salary threshold alone cannot determine priorities because housing costs, dependants, benefits, debt, and province can change the outcome.

Situation Where to focus What could change the order
Low income or little room after essentials File to access eligible benefits, stabilize bills and build a starter reserve. Consider a TFSA for accessible savings if there is room. Evaluate a feasible match and benefit effects. Check eligibility for the Canada Workers Benefit, CGEB and provincial support.
Moderate income with steady surplus Compare FHSA for an eligible home purchase, an employer match, and TFSA/RRSP for the remaining goals. Use estimated effective rates today and at withdrawal. Child benefits can make a deduction more valuable than the headline bracket suggests.
High income or an unusually strong earnings year Compare RRSP deduction timing, fill suitable registered room and use a taxable account for additional investing when needed. A substantial pension or high future income may raise withdrawal rates. Model tax on concentrated assets before selling.
Single earner or irregular income Hold a larger liquidity buffer if an interruption would threaten essential bills. Review disability coverage and tax reserves. A strong pension, stable second income or accessible savings may change the reserve target.
Couple or family with dependants Make one household budget and track each person’s registered room. Review life and disability coverage, CCB and education savings. Employer benefits, parental leave and childcare can change cash flow. Each eligible partner can open their own FHSA.
First-time buyer with a fixed purchase date Calculate down payment, closing costs and cash needed after closing. Compare eligible FHSA and TFSA savings with HBP cash flow. A purchase delay, ownership history, lender requirements or an HBP repayment obligation may change the plan.
Family saving for education or disability Compare RESP grants and RDSP grants or bonds with the household’s immediate needs. Basic CESG generally adds 20% up to $500 annually per eligible beneficiary, subject to carryforward, age rules and a $7,200 lifetime cap. Check the Canada Learning Bond if eligible and RDSP grant or bond repayment rules before withdrawing.
Near retirement or expecting low retirement income Estimate CPP/QPP, OAS and GIS, then compare withdrawal plans using after-tax income and benefits. GIS eligibility, a pension bridge ending or survivor income can change the best year to draw registered funds.

Investing by time horizon

When you need the money Approach to compare Main risk to manage
Within about three years, with a firm date Accessible insured savings, or eligible GICs that mature before the goal. A market decline, withdrawal restriction or maturity after the due date.
Roughly three to seven years Keep any required amount in deposits or other holdings appropriate for the deadline. Consider diversified investments for money whose spending date or amount can move. Stocks can fall sharply and bond funds can lose value. A flexible date matters.
Longer than seven years Compare a broad mix of Canadian and global equities, high-quality bonds and cash. A diversified all-in-one fund, managed portfolio or adviser-supported portfolio may fit. Concentration, high costs, selling during a downturn and choosing more risk than the household can absorb.

Set the allocation by risk capacity (how much loss your finances can absorb) and risk tolerance (how much volatility you can live with). Use the more restrictive result. Write down the goal, target allocation, contribution schedule, and annual review or rebalancing rule. Use new contributions to rebalance where practical, and consider taxes or fees before selling. Compare fund expenses, advice fees, commissions and currency costs. Verify adviser registration and scope. CDIC insures eligible deposits. CIPF coverage can apply to eligible property missing after a member firm’s insolvency. Market declines are outside CIPF protection. [30] [31] [5] [32]

Worked fee example. A $50,000 lump sum growing for 20 years at an assumed 5% gross annual return reaches about $126,488 at a 0.25% annual fee or $94,792 at a 1.75% fee. The difference is roughly $31,696. This illustration subtracts the fee from the annual return and assumes constant returns, no contributions, no taxes and no inflation. It isolates fee drag, the amount costs reduce growth. It is a projection under assumptions.

Before buying checklist: ☐ Verify time horizon, acceptable loss and liquidity. ☐ Read the fee and risk disclosure. ☐ Check existing employer, home and portfolio concentration. ☐ Verify the provider and applicable deposit or investor protection. ☐ Confirm the contribution and withdrawal rules.

Insurance and financial risk

Start with losses the household could not absorb. Compare employer and private coverage together. Read exclusions, deductibles, waiting periods, benefit caps, tax treatment, portability and the claims procedure. Review coverage after a move, new dependant, mortgage, job change or separation. Insurance and estate requirements vary by province. [33] [34]

Risk What to check How to size the gap
Death of an income earner or caregiver Existing group and individual life insurance, beneficiaries, conversion and term expiry. Add debts, childcare, income replacement and one-time costs. Subtract available assets and coverage. Compare term insurance for time-limited needs.
Illness or disability reduces income Employer short- and long-term disability, EI or other eligible coverage, waiting period, definition of disability, offsets, exclusions and taxable benefits. Subtract expected net benefit from essential spending. Estimate how long cash would bridge the waiting period.
Home, belongings or liability loss Tenant, condo or home insurance, replacement cost, personal liability, deductibles, and exclusions or endorsements for water, earthquake and other relevant risks. Match policy terms to location and property exposure. Include strata deductibles where relevant.
Financial fraud and account access Use unique passwords and multi-factor authentication. Turn on alerts. Confirm payment requests through a known number. Review credit reports and dispute unfamiliar entries promptly. Document contact numbers and recovery steps in a secure place. Verify investment registration before sending money.
Incapacity or death administration Will, appropriate powers of attorney or provincial equivalents, and beneficiary and successor-holder designations where available. Identify who can act, which bills must continue and where documents are kept. Get province-specific advice for complex families or assets.

Worked life-cover estimate. Suppose a family wants $300,000 to reduce debts, $40,000 a year for eight years of support and $30,000 for one-time costs, with $150,000 of accessible assets or existing coverage. The simple gap is $500,000. That estimate ignores inflation, investment returns, taxes and benefit changes. Check term length, survivor income and whether existing coverage ends with employment. [34] [35]

Stress test Recalculate If the plan falls short
Lose an income for six months Months of essential bills covered after confirmed net benefits. Reduce discretionary outflows, build more accessible cash and check benefit eligibility.
Mortgage renews 2 percentage points higher Required payment, then remaining monthly surplus. Compare renewal options early and check when prepayment is permitted.
Stocks fall 30% when you need cash Cash available without selling, and the portfolio’s dollar loss. Reduce dependence on stock sales for fixed near-term spending.
Inflation rises or retirement lasts longer After-tax spending gap using higher expenses and a later planning age. Compare flexible spending, pension start dates and other income sources.

Retirement income and withdrawals

  1. Estimate essential and flexible spending in today’s dollars. Include housing, taxes, irregular costs and health or care expenses.
  2. Get personal CPP or QPP estimates, employer pension statements and OAS eligibility. Confirm inflation adjustments, bridge benefits, survivor terms and the timing of payments. Run single-survivor cash flow for couples.
  3. Compare benefit start dates and account withdrawals year by year. CPP can begin from 60 to 70. Compared with age 65, the monthly adjustment is −0.6% per month before 65, up to 36%, or +0.7% per month after 65, up to 42%. Québec’s QPP has separate rules and can increase through age 72. OAS can start at 65 and rises 0.6% per month if deferred, up to 36% at age 70. While OAS is deferred you cannot receive GIS. Check OAS residency, OAS recovery tax and GIS income tests before choosing.
  4. Compare drawing from TFSA, taxable accounts and RRSP/RRIF in different years. Early taxable withdrawals can sometimes smooth taxes. They can also affect benefits. An RRSP generally must mature by December 31 of the year you turn 71, with options including transferring to a RRIF, buying an eligible annuity or taking a taxable withdrawal. RRIF minimum withdrawals generally start the following year.
  5. Test poor returns early in retirement, higher inflation, and living to 95 or beyond. Early losses matter when withdrawals force you to sell assets at lower prices. Compare several spending paths before committing.

Use the Canadian Retirement Income Calculator and OAS Benefits Estimator for starting estimates, then model household taxes and income-tested benefits. [36] [37] [38] [39] [40] [29] [15] [41]

Worked retirement example. At $48,000 annual after-tax spending and $30,000 net pension and public benefits, savings must provide $18,000 a year. From $450,000 of after-tax-equivalent assets, that is 4% in the first year. A 20% drop before withdrawals would reduce assets to $360,000 and raise the same draw to 5%. For an ordinary RRSP withdrawal at a flat 25% effective rate, $18,000 net instead requires $24,000 gross. This is a stress test. A sustainable draw depends on longevity, investment returns, fees, inflation, taxes and spending flexibility.

2027 rules and figures to verify

Evidence checked November 11, 2026. Published figures come from official sources. Examples in this playbook use illustrative rates, costs and returns. They are scenarios, with no projected 2027 TFSA limit, income brackets, or market return.

Topic Status at the evidence date What to do
RRSP ceiling Published for 2027: $35,390, up from $33,810 for 2026. Your personal limit depends on your earnings and pension adjustments. Check your 2026 Notice of Assessment or Reassessment and contributions already made. [42] [13]
TFSA annual limit 2027 figure unverified: CRA’s table reviewed lists $7,000 for 2026 and ends there. Check CRA’s updated table before relying on new room. Include unused prior room and eligible 2026 withdrawals, and reconcile all providers. [42] [10]
Income tax brackets and credits 2027 federal figures unverified in the CRA rate table reviewed. Provinces set their own rules. B.C., for example, says it paused bracket indexation for 2027–2030. Look up 2027 federal and provincial brackets, basic personal amounts and applicable credits. Use 2026 rules for 2026 income. [22] [43]
CPP and EI Published for 2027: CPP pensionable-earnings ceilings of $76,700 and $87,400. The first-tier employee CPP rate is 5.75% above the $3,500 exemption, up to the first ceiling. EI maximum insurable earnings are $70,800. Employee EI is 1.64% outside Quebec and 1.29% in Quebec. Update the take-home-pay budget. In Quebec use QPP, Quebec EI and Quebec Parental Insurance Plan rules where applicable. [42] [44] [45] [46]
Home Buyers’ Plan CRA guidance: fifth-year repayment start for first withdrawals from 2022 through 2028. A 2027 first withdrawal points to 2032. Verify eligibility, the withdrawal date, and the repayment amount in CRA records before relying on it. [18] [19]
Federal filing and balance-due date Budget 2026 proposal: May 15 for individuals starting with the 2027 tax year, normally filed in 2028. Enactment was unverified at the evidence date. Use CRA’s published 2027 filing-season deadlines for 2026 returns. Check legislation and CRA before filing 2027 returns. [47] [4] [48]
Tax instalments 2027 dates published for affected taxpayers. Budget 2026 proposes higher thresholds and a single annual option for certain business earners starting in 2028. Follow applicable 2027 dates and your CRA instructions. Quebec residents should also check provincial obligations. [47] [4]
Benefits Annual or quarterly updates: CCB, CGEB, GIS and OAS payment amounts or eligibility thresholds can change. Run current federal and provincial estimators using family income, age, residency and other eligibility inputs. [24] [49] [23] [29]
Capital gains Current law reviewed: section 38 generally applies a one-half inclusion rate, subject to exceptions. A proposed change, special exemption or transaction-specific rule can change the calculation. Recheck the current statute and CRA instructions before a significant sale. Keep cost and sale records. [21] [20]

12-month action plan

Month Action Record when complete
January Pull statements, calculate net worth and essential spending, verify 2027 room and set payday transfers. Starting net worth, monthly surplus, reserve target and next priority.
February Review 2026 slips as they arrive and compare any RRSP contribution for the 2026 return with cash needs and your personal limit. Intended contribution and deduction year. Confirm the CRA deadline before sending funds.
March Update debt rates and minimums. Set a payment schedule. If tax instalments apply, check the March 15 due date. Target payoff date, required instalment and current balance.
April File the 2026 return by April 30 for most filers and pay any balance due by April 30 in the usual case, including most self-employed filers. Return and payment confirmations, refund or balance allocation.
May Compare investments and fees. Verify your institution or adviser and write down the portfolio rules. Allocation, all-in cost estimate and review date.
June Check the June 15 filing deadline for eligible self-employed 2026 filers and the June 15 instalment date if applicable. Review insurance. Coverage gaps, renewal dates and proof of filing or payment.
July Update the budget with current benefits, household changes and midyear actuals. Check your monthly reserve progress. Revised monthly surplus and benefit estimates.
August Update home, education or other dated goals. Check contribution room and grant eligibility. Target cost, deadline and monthly amount for each goal.
September Get pension and public-benefit estimates. Model retirement or a major income interruption. Check the September 15 instalment date if applicable. After-tax retirement gap and reserve shortfall under stress.
October Check renewals and major bills due in the next six months. Review beneficiaries, wills and powers of attorney. Renewal quotes or dates and any estate documents to update.
November Estimate 2027 taxable income and benefits. Compare timing of deductions, gains or withdrawals. Tax estimate, updated contribution ledger and decisions to execute.
December If applicable, check the December 15 instalment. Confirm issuer cutoffs for calendar-year TFSA and FHSA transactions. Review results and set 2028 priorities. Year-end account and debt balances, room records and next year’s first action.

Tax dates refer to the 2027 calendar year. April and June filing dates above apply to the 2026 tax return. Check exceptions and applicable provincial filing or instalment requirements. [48] [4]

Inputs needed for an individualized plan

Input Why it changes the recommendation
Province or territory, tax residency history and expected moves Changes rates, benefits, account eligibility and potentially cross-border treatment.
Ages, marital or common-law status, dependants and caregiving Determines benefit eligibility, household risk, home-buyer tests and planning horizon.
Gross and net income by person, sources, variability, expected changes and current benefits Sets spending capacity, marginal tax rates, potential benefit reductions and tax timing.
Essential, irregular and optional expenses Sizes emergency reserves and tests whether planned contributions are sustainable.
Debts, balances, rates, payment terms, renewal dates and penalties Determines payoff order, refinancing economics and liquidity risk.
Assets by account, holdings, fees, taxable cost base, available room, and undeducted contributions Determines account capacity, investment concentration and taxes on sales or withdrawals.
Employer match, pension and insurance terms, CPP/QPP estimates and OAS residency years Changes contribution priority, income protection, benefit start dates and retirement funding.
Homeownership history, target date and expected cost Tests FHSA/HBP eligibility and determines how much of the down payment must stay liquid.
Other goals, target dates, minimum amounts and flexibility Determines funding order and the appropriate holdings for each goal.
Risk tolerance, loss capacity and ability to stick to a plan in a downturn Sets an investment mix the household can afford and maintain.

Rounded figures are enough for a first pass. Keep Social Insurance Numbers, account numbers, passwords and full statements out of a planning intake. Before implementing a personalized plan, verify contribution room, tax calculations, benefit eligibility and contract terms against your records.
